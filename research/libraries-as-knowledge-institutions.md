# Libraries as knowledge institutions: a model for agentic knowledge bases

Produced 2026-07-25 via four parallelized sub-research passes (validation & curation, classification & cataloging, preservation & deaccessioning, access & reference service), grounded in ALA/RUSA guidelines, IFLA/LOC standards, and peer-reviewed and trade LIS literature — full citations inline and in Sources. Scope is library science specifically, not archives-as-institution or museums; "archive" elsewhere in this repo refers to an artifact type (a memo, ledger, or note), not this separate institutional model.

**how to apply:** consult when designing how an agentic system should decide what to persist, how to organize it for retrieval, when to retire it, and how to surface it on demand.

## 1. Validation & curation: deciding what enters the collection

Every major LIS authority treats a written collection development policy (CDP) as a professional baseline, not an option — ALA states "every library... should have a comprehensive written policy," reviewed regularly ([ALA Selection Criteria](https://www.ala.org/tools/challengesupport/selectionpolicytoolkit/criteria)). ALA's criteria fall into three tiers: *general* (relevance to community needs, cost, relation to the existing collection, review attention, patron demand), *content* (authority/competence of the creator, accuracy, currency, comprehensiveness, viewpoint diversity), and *format-specific* (durability, access requirements) — the same list university policies re-operationalize under different names, often favoring "just-in-time" demand-driven acquisition over speculative collecting ([WVU Libraries CDP](https://library.wvu.edu/collections/collection-development-policy)). The **Conspectus model** (RLG, 1979; adopted by ARL) formalizes *depth* of curation per subject on a 0–5 scale from Minimal to Comprehensive ([IFLA Conspectus Guidelines](https://repository.ifla.org/items/a50d5da4-16a0-4713-afbd-530a572fc442)) — not every topic warrants the same collecting intensity.

There are two distinct validation layers, not one. **Peer review** is an external, pre-acquisition filter performed by subject experts before a library ever sees a scholarly item — "the only universally accepted method for validating and assessing the quality of articles" prior to publication ([Wikipedia: Scholarly peer review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholarly_peer_review)). For general/trade material with no such upstream gate, librarians substitute their own judgment, heavily mediated by trade review sources (*Booklist*, *Kirkus*, *Library Journal*) ([ALA: Reviews as a Selection Tool](https://libguides.ala.org/marketing-to-libraries/reviews)). The closest thing to a unifying philosophy is Ranganathan's **Five Laws of Library Science** (1931) — "Books are for use," "Every reader his/her book," "Every book its reader," "Save the time of the reader," "The library is a growing organism" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_laws_of_library_science)) — a normative orientation toward serving actual reader need, not the selector's own taste.

This is a live, unresolved tension, not a solved problem: ALA's own Library Bill of Rights simultaneously requires that libraries not exclude material for its viewpoint *and* actively curate/weed for accuracy and relevance ([Library Bill of Rights](https://www.ala.org/advocacy/intfreedom/librarybill); [Evaluating Library Collections](https://www.ala.org/advocacy/intfreedom/librarybill/interpretations/evaluatinglibrary)). Scholarly critique goes further — Jahnke, Tanaka & Palazzolo argue the very tools of "neutral" curation (use statistics, standardized metadata, vendor packages) systematically under-select marginalized and non-mainstream material, so professional neutrality itself reproduces exclusion ([*College & Research Libraries*](https://crl.acrl.org/index.php/crl/article/view/25342/33226)).

**Agentic analogy:** "is this worth persisting as a memo" maps to selection criteria layer for layer — authority/accuracy → is the claim corroborated by checkable sources; relevance/scope → does it serve the repo's actual ongoing questions, not tangential curiosity; duplication → does an existing memo already cover this finding; currency → is this a durable fact or a transient, session-specific detail. The peer-review/trade-review split maps to distinguishing claims the agent can ground against primary sources (high confidence) from claims resting on its own synthesis or a single weak source (needing an explicit, flagged judgment call rather than false certainty). The unresolved tension carries over too: an agent enforcing strict "worth persisting" criteria is itself a gatekeeper, and an overly mechanical filter risks under-persisting exactly the novel or contrarian findings that don't yet have easy corroboration.

## 2. Classification & cataloging: organizing for retrieval, not just storage

Two enumerative, hierarchical systems dominate physical shelving. **Dewey Decimal Classification** (1876, OCLC-maintained) divides all knowledge into ten main classes with decimal expansion — simple, numeric, shelf-mappable, dominant in public libraries. **Library of Congress Classification** uses 21 alphabetic main classes built pragmatically class-by-class rather than from one master hierarchy — more granular, dominant in academic/research libraries ([comparison](https://www.librarianshipstudies.com/2026/01/comparison-between-dewey-decimal.html); [LCC Outline](https://www.loc.gov/catdir/cpso/lcco/lccowp.html)). Both force a single, fixed "home" per item because a physical book can only sit on one shelf.

Ranganathan's **Colon Classification** (1933) broke that constraint with **faceted classification**: rather than enumerating every subject in advance, a subject is analyzed into up to five categories — **P**ersonality, **M**atter, **E**nergy, **S**pace, **T**ime (PMEST) — and a class number is *synthesized* by combining facets, not looked up whole ([PMEST overview](https://www.lisedunetwork.com/ranganathans-pmest-the-foundation-of-faceted-classification/)). This is the conceptual ancestor of faceted search/filtering: a subject as independently-combinable dimensions rather than one shelf address.

On metadata, **MARC** (Library of Congress, from the 1960s; MARC 21 today) optimizes for depth within the library domain — richly structured bibliographic fields for title, author, subjects, physical description, holdings ([LC MARC 21](https://www.loc.gov/marc/bibliographic/)). **Dublin Core** takes the opposite bet: 15 general-purpose elements (Title, Creator, Subject, Description, Date, Type, ...) simple enough for non-catalogers and interoperable across domains, trading depth for cross-domain simplicity ([DCMI Metadata Terms](https://www.dublincore.org/specifications/dublin-core/dcmi-terms/)).

On vocabulary, **controlled vocabularies** like Library of Congress Subject Headings (1898–) fix one preferred term per concept with cross-referenced synonyms, giving consistent collocation at the cost of expensive, slow-to-update cataloger labor ([LCSH](https://id.loc.gov/authorities/subjects.html)). **Folksonomies** — Thomas Vander Wal's 2004 term for bottom-up, user-created free tagging — are cheap, immediate, and reflect real usage, but suffer synonymy, polysemy, and no synonym control. Elaine Peterson's critique goes further, arguing folksonomy's relativism permits logically contradictory tags on the same item and accumulates "meta noise" at scale, while conceding real advantages in cost, speed, and democratized participation ([Peterson, D-Lib Magazine 2006](https://www.dlib.org/dlib/november06/peterson/11peterson.html)). LIS literature leans toward coexistence — controlled vocabulary for formal, high-stakes retrieval; folksonomy for informal, fast-moving, high-volume retrieval — not a verdict that one wins outright ([Folksonomies: (Un)Controlled Vocabulary?](http://eprints.rclis.org/10307/)).

**Agentic analogy:** a flat file-per-memo repo with a single index (as sintesis is today) is structurally pre-classification — it has cataloging (an index pointing to items) but no classification scheme. Faceted classification maps onto structured frontmatter: instead of one enumerated "shelf position" per memo, PMEST-style facets (topic, status, project, date, audience) could be independently combinable and filterable — synthesis instead of a single folder. Controlled vocabulary vs. folksonomy is the tag-value question: a small, curated, fixed vocabulary avoids "agentic-orchestration" vs. "agent-orchestration" vs. "multi-agent-coordination" all meaning the same thing but being unfindable together, at the cost of someone maintaining that taxonomy as topics emerge; free-text descriptions are the folksonomy end — cheap and current, but will accumulate the same synonymy/polysemy drift once memo count outgrows what one retrieving agent can hold in mind.

## 3. Preservation & deaccessioning: what's kept, what's let go, and how

"Weeding" is standard terminology for removing items from a *circulating* collection, not an archive. The most cited practical framework, **CREW** ("Continuous Review, Evaluation, and Weeding," Texas State Library, 1976, rev. 2012), uses the **MUSTIE** mnemonic: **M**isleading, **U**gly, **S**uperseded, **T**rivial, **I**rrelevant, **E**lsewhere-available ([CREW manual excerpt](https://yln.libguides.com/c.php?g=1077306&p=7958012)). Notably, TSLAC has itself archived the CREW manual as of 2025 with no plans to update it — a case of the phenomenon documenting itself.

Libraries structurally separate circulating collections (weeded routinely) from permanent ones. Legal/copyright deposit — the UK's six legal deposit libraries, the US Copyright Office's mandatory deposit to the Library of Congress — guarantees at least one permanent copy of everything published ([Legal Deposit Libraries Act 2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_Deposit_Libraries_Act_2003); [US mandatory deposit](https://www.copyright.gov/mandatory/)). Where academic libraries do withdraw duplicates, consortial "last copy" and shared-print programs (e.g. [CARLI Last Copy Program](https://www.carli.illinois.edu/products-services/collections-management/last-copy-project)) act as a safety net with fixed retention commitments (commonly 25 years), so at least one copy survives across the consortium even as individual libraries deaccession theirs.

Versioning is handled by never deleting the record of an earlier edition — the **FRBR/IFLA-LRM** model formalizes this as **WEMI**: Work → Expression → Manifestation → Item ([IFLA LRM](https://www.ifla.org/files/assets/cataloguing/frbr-lrm/ifla-lrm-august-2017_rev201712.pdf)). A revised edition is a new Expression/Manifestation linked back to the same Work; both stay addressable — nothing is destroyed, only related. Digitally, the **LOCKSS** program ("Lots Of Copies Keep Stuff Safe," Stanford, 1999) replicates content peer-to-peer with no single point of control, and **CLOCKSS** runs the same technology as a shared dark archive across 12 global nodes, releasing content openly only when a publisher ceases to exist or content becomes unavailable ([LOCKSS](https://www.lockss.org/about/preservation-principles); [CLOCKSS FAQ](https://clockss.org/faq/)).

Weeding remains genuinely contentious in practice, not just in theory: Berkeley Public Library's 2015 weeding project discarded ~39,000 books, including 13,000+ "last copy" unique titles, and provoked protests and a whistleblower controversy ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/sanfrancisco/news/berkeley-residents-peeved-over-mysterious-loss-of-thousands-of-public-library-books); [Library Journal](https://www.libraryjournal.com/story/the-weeding-war-blatant-berry)) — a reminder that "superseded" and "irrelevant" are judgment calls people reasonably disagree about, and any given discard is irreversible for that copy.

**Agentic analogy:** the "circulating collection" is whatever memos are actively surfaced/consulted; MUSTIE-style criteria (superseded, factually stale, no longer relevant) are the librarian's judgment call before removal. But this repo's own convention — never mark a memo superseded by changes still unmerged to main — is more conservative than public-library weeding: don't relabel the 1st edition until the 2nd is verified and landed, exactly mirroring how a library doesn't discard an edition until its successor is cataloged. Git history supplies the FRBR backbone for free: each commit is an Expression of the memo-as-Work, `git log`/`git blame` preserve every prior Manifestation without deletion, and a forward-pointing supersession note is an LRM "successor of" relationship. Where CREW's *E for Elsewhere* justifies discarding a physical duplicate because content survives elsewhere, in a git repo nothing committed is ever truly gone — version control already gives every memo the LOCKSS property (redundant, checksummed copies across clones/remotes) and the legal-deposit property (a permanent record outliving whichever copy is currently "circulating," i.e. actively linked to).

## 4. Access & reference service: surfacing the right thing at the right moment

The **reference interview** is the formal technique for translating a patron's stated question into their actual information need; RUSA's behavioral guidelines call it "the heart of the reference transaction" ([RUSA Guidelines](https://www.ala.org/sites/default/files/rusa/content/Guidelines%20for%20Behavioral%20Performance%20of%20Reference%20and%20Information%20Service%20Providers%202023.pdf)). The theoretical grounding is Robert Taylor's 1968 paper on four levels of information need: **visceral** (unformed sense something is missing), **conscious** (still-ambiguous mental articulation), **formalized** (a qualified, rational statement), and **compromised** (the question actually asked, reshaped by what the user expects the system can answer) ([Taylor 1968](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/38316); retrospective by [Tyckoson 2015](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1058189)). The librarian's job is to negotiate backward from the compromised question toward the formalized need — not answer the literal string.

Findability rests on parallel systems: subject headings/OPAC search for keyword retrieval, and call-number shelf order for *browsable* discovery — patrons searching for one item routinely find adjacent items shelved nearby ([shelf browsing and serendipity](https://www.library.jhu.edu/news/2013/10/browsing-serendipity-and-virtual-discovery/)). But catalogs are self-service tools requiring the patron to already know the right terminology, which is exactly the gap a reference librarian closes by running the search themselves rather than pointing at the catalog — RUSA's "searching" behavior formalizes this as "developing competent search strategies and working collaboratively with patrons."

Ray Oldenburg's "third place" concept — social space distinct from home and work — has been applied directly to libraries: Given & Leckie's observational study documented patrons using central public libraries as sustained-use space, not transactional stops ([Given & Leckie 2003](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222684178_Abstract_%27%27Sweeping%27_%27_the_library_Mapping_the_social_activity_space_of_the_public_library_1)). This is structurally different from a lookup: a destination for dwell time, browsable stacks inviting serendipity, and the presence of other readers validating sustained study as legitimate rather than merely tolerated.

There's a live debate over whether self-service digital discovery is displacing mediated reference — reference transaction counts fell sharply as users shifted to Google/Google Scholar — but Bandyopadhyay & Boyd-Byrnes's direct study concludes it is *not* fading: "professional librarians' skills, knowledge and expertise are essential" in the digital environment ([Reference Services Review 2016](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/rsr-02-2016-0012/full/html)). The same debate recurs in current AI-chatbot-reference literature, generally converging on chatbots-as-complement (handling routine, 24/7 queries) rather than chatbots-as-replacement for the judgment-heavy interview.

**Agentic analogy:** Taylor's four levels map directly onto how an agent should approach an indexed memo repository — rather than grep-matching a user's literal keywords against filenames or content (a raw OPAC query returning whatever surface-matches), the agent should read the index, identify which memos are topically relevant to the *underlying* question, and open several before synthesizing — the reference interview, not the call-number lookup. And the third-place framing argues the knowledge base should support browsing, not just point answers: an agent that returns a single grep hit forecloses the adjacent-memo discovery a shelf-browsing patron gets for free.

## Design principles for an agentic knowledge base

Pulling the four sections together, the library model implies:

1. **Write down selection criteria, don't rely on ad hoc judgment.** A CDP-equivalent — even an informal one — for "what's worth persisting" (authority, relevance, non-duplication, durability) makes the gatekeeping decision legible and revisable, rather than a private judgment call repeated inconsistently every session.
2. **Distinguish grounded claims from synthesized judgment**, the way peer-reviewed material is distinguished from trade-reviewed material — flag which memo claims are corroborated against primary sources versus resting on the writer's own synthesis.
3. **Classify by combinable facets, not a single enumerated slot**, once a flat index stops scaling — faceted (PMEST-style) metadata composes; a single category tree forces awkward one-shelf-per-item choices.
4. **Pick controlled vocabulary deliberately, not by default** — a small maintained tag set buys collocation and retrievability at the cost of upkeep; free-text tags are cheap and current but will drift into synonymy/noise as volume grows. Match the choice to how much retrieval precision the collection actually needs yet.
5. **Separate "circulating" from "permanent," and never destroy provenance when superseding** — mark newer material as superseding older material via an explicit forward link (an LRM "successor of" relationship), not by deleting or silently overwriting; git already gives this for free.
6. **Treat retrieval as an active reference interview, not a passive index lookup** — negotiate from the literal question toward the underlying need, consult multiple related memos, and support browsing/serendipity rather than returning a single best-match grep hit.
7. **Expect the gatekeeping/openness tension to stay unresolved, not solved** — strict selection criteria risk under-persisting novel, hard-to-corroborate findings the same way library "neutrality" criteria have been shown to under-select non-mainstream material; there's no criteria set that avoids this tradeoff entirely, only ones that make the tradeoff visible.
